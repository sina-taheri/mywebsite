The following is an excerpt from a longer essay that I wrote a while back, it is by no means complete (and was not supposed to be so) but partly manifests my point of view about what I value when I'm lucky enough to be on the other side of the classroom. The list of teaching experiences that comes after, all of them (or most depending on when you read this!) were during my time at the department of Mathematics and Statistics of Concordia University.

> <span style="font-size: 10px;">I firmly believe that education should be accessible to everyone, regardless of their background, ethnicity, race, gender, economic or social class, etc. Unfortunately, history (and current time) shows that mankind has struggled to achieve this ideal and it is partly on our shoulders to remove these barriers and create opportunities for all...</span>
>
> <span style="font-size: 10px;">Every class from the moment it begins until the very final minute—and even beyond class hours—is like a movie scene or play, and a teacher takes on the roles of a playwrighter and narrator, weaving the storyline with care. Each course topic serves like a distinct scene, and a teacher who is skilled in the subtle art of story-telling puts these scenes together, and guides students in a captivating tour. He builds suspense, challenge, and finally brings joy by allowing students experience “the pleasure of discovery”. Teachers can also bring their own unique colors to this narrative, shaped by their expertise and interests. When it comes to Mathematics, my favorite “color” is the history of mathematics. I have an ongoing interest in this subject, and whenever time and content allow, I incorporate historical elements into my teaching. Mathematics as the “language of the universe” (if you believe so?), possesses a logical beauty that drives arguments and builds a solid narrative. However, like any language, mathematics requires careful interpretation, otherwise the gist of contents can be lost in the formality of the language, that has been developed over time for the sake of rigor and universality. Through this perspective, the goal of teaching mathematics is to help students become at ease with this language and the ideas it carries. By achieving this, students can apply mathematical concepts effectively, and build a foundation for the rest of their learning journey. The teacher, however, should design clear and measurable metrics to assess to what extent this goal is achieved. Assessments should target students’ long-term understanding and conceptual knowledge rather than memorization that fades a while later.</span>
